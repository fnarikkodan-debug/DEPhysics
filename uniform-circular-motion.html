Same speed, changing direction

Uniform circular motion (UCM) means an object travels around a circle at a constant speed. The speed never changes, but the direction changes at every moment.

Key idea Constant speed does not mean constant velocity. Velocity includes direction. In a circle the direction keeps turning, so velocity keeps changing, and a changing velocity means the object is accelerating.

Velocity always points along the tangent, the straight line that just touches the circle at the ball's location.

Acceleration always points toward the center. It's called centripetal acceleration, which means "center-seeking."

Each arrow stays the same length as the ball goes around. The arrows only rotate.

Before you can compute forces, you need to describe how fast the object goes around. Physicists usually start from the time it takes to make one lap.

Where v = 2πr / T comes from

Speed is distance divided by time. In one period T, the object goes around once, a distance equal to the circumference 2πr.

v = distancetime = 2πrT

Unit tip: rpm Motors and fans are often rated in revolutions per minute. Divide by 60 to get frequency: 1200 rpm ÷ 60 = 20 rev/s, so T = 1/20 = 0.05 s.
Frequencyf = 1T

Short period means high frequency. They are reciprocals.

Speedv = 2πrT

The equation you'll use most often in this unit.

Same equation, written with frequency.

Used in some courses and in rotational motion later.

r = 0.60 m3 rev/s

The blade tip moves at ≈ 11 m/s, about 25 mph. Points closer to the center move slower because their circle is smaller.

Centripetal acceleration points to the center

Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by time. To find its direction, compare the velocity at two nearby moments and look at the difference, Δv.

The ball moves counterclockwise from the earlier point to the later one. Its velocity turns from v₁ to v₂. Both arrows have the same length because the speed is the same.

Draw them tail to tail. The arrow that goes from the tip of v₁ to the tip of v₂ is Δv, and it points straight toward the center of the circle.

Since a = Δv / Δt, the acceleration points the same way as Δv: toward the center. This is true at every point on the circle.

Centripetal accelerationac = v²r

Use this when you know the speed.

Using periodac = 4π²rT²

Substitute v = 2πr/T into v²/r.

Extension: angularac = ω²r

Same idea, written with angular speed.

What if you let go?

Cut the string and the inward pull disappears. With no net force, Newton's first law says the ball keeps its velocity at that instant, so it moves in a straight line along the tangent.

It does not fly straight outward. Nothing pushes it out. The circle only happens because something keeps pulling inward.

Centripetal force: a job, not a new force

Newton's second law (F = ma) says an inward acceleration needs an inward net force.

Newton's 2nd law, toward the center ΣFin = mv²r

Add up the real forces pointing toward the center (minus any pointing away). That total must equal mv²/r.

The most important rule in this unit "Centripetal force" is the name of a job, like "goalkeeper." Some real force has to do the job: tension, friction, gravity or a normal force. Never draw a separate arrow labeled Fc on a free-body diagram.

Who does the job? Four free-body diagrams

The "centrifugal force" myth

In a car taking a sharp left turn, you feel thrown to the right. No outward force is acting on you.

Your body has inertia and keeps moving straight (dashed line). The car turns under you, so the door comes toward you and pushes you inward. That inward push is the centripetal force acting on you.

In an inertial (non-accelerating) frame, which is the frame used in this course, there is no outward force to draw.

Why speeding through a curve is dangerous

Because F = mv²/r, the inward force needed grows with the square of speed.

Going around a curve at twice the speed needs four times the friction. If the road can't supply it (ice, gravel, worn tires), the car can't stay on the curve and slides off along a straighter path.

Tighter curves (smaller r) also need more force at the same speed. That's why sharp turns have lower speed limits.

Worked examples

Use the same six steps on every circular motion problem.

  1. Sketch the circle and mark the center.
  2. Draw a free-body diagram with real forces only.
  3. Choose toward the center as the positive direction.
  4. If you're given the period, find speed first: v = 2πr/T.
  5. Write ΣFin = mv²/r.
  6. Solve, then check that the units and size of the answer make sense.

Example 1: Ball on a string

A 0.50 kg ball is swung in a horizontal circle of radius 1.2 m. It makes one revolution every 0.80 s. Find its speed, its acceleration, and the tension in the string.

m = 0.50 kgr = 1.2 mT = 0.80 s
  • Speed from the periodv = 2πr/T = 2π(1.2)/0.80 = 9.42 m/s
  • Centripetal accelerationa = v²/r = (9.42)²/1.2 = 74.0 m/s² (toward the center)
  • Tension is the only inward forceTstring = ma = 0.50 × 74.0 = 37 N

Answer: v = 9.4 m/s, a = 74 m/s², tension = 37 N. That acceleration is about 7.5 g.

Example 2: Car on a flat curve

A 1200 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 50 m at 15 m/s. How much friction is needed? What is the minimum coefficient of static friction?

m = 1200 kgr = 50 mv = 15 m/sg = 9.8 m/s²
  • Friction does the centripetal jobf = mv²/r = 1200 × 15² / 50 = 5400 N
  • Maximum friction available is μN = μmgμmg = mv²/r → μ = v²/(rg)
  • Mass cancelsμ = 225 / (50 × 9.8) = 0.46

Answer: 5400 N of friction, so μ ≥ 0.46. Dry asphalt (μ ≈ 0.7) works. Ice (μ ≈ 0.1) does not.

Example 3: Top of a roller-coaster loop

What is the slowest a cart can go at the top of a loop of radius 10 m and still stay on the track?

r = 10 mg = 9.8 m/s²
  • At the top, both N and mg point down, toward the centerN + mg = mv²/r
  • Slowest case: the track barely touches, so N = 0mg = mv²/r → v = √(gr)
  • Plug inv = √(9.8 × 10) = 9.9 m/s

Answer: vmin ≈ 9.9 m/s. Any slower and gravity pulls the cart down more than the circle needs, so it leaves the track.

Note: speed changes as the cart climbs, so the whole loop isn't uniform motion. We apply F = mv²/r at one point.

Question 1

A child sits 2.0 m from the center of a merry-go-round that makes one revolution every 4.0 s. What is the child's speed in m/s?

m/s
Show solution

v = 2πr/T = 2π(2.0)/4.0 = 3.14 m/s

Question 2

What is that child's centripetal acceleration in m/s²?

m/s²
Show solution

a = v²/r = (3.14)²/2.0 = 4.93 m/s², directed toward the center.

Question 3

What is the minimum speed at the top of a roller-coaster loop of radius 10 m?

m/s
Show solution

At minimum speed N = 0, so mg = mv²/r and v = √(gr) = √(98) = 9.9 m/s.

Question 4

A ball on a string moves in a circle. If its speed triples and the radius stays the same, the tension becomes how many times larger?

times
Show solution

Tension = mv²/r, so it scales with v². 3² = 9 times larger.

Question 5

A fan spins at 1200 rpm. Its blade tips are 0.25 m from the axle. How fast do the tips move, in m/s?

m/s
Show solution

f = 1200/60 = 20 rev/s, so T = 0.05 s. v = 2π(0.25)/0.05 = 31.4 m/s.

Question 6

An object is in uniform circular motion. The net force on it is…

Show solution

Velocity is changing direction, so there is acceleration toward the center, and by F = ma the net force also points toward the center.

Question 7

You are swinging a ball on a string and let go. Right after release, the ball moves…

Show solution

With the inward force gone, Newton's first law keeps the velocity it had at that instant, which points along the tangent.

Question 8 · Worksheet #22 · Vertical circle

A 0.50 kg ball is attached to a string 1.20 m long and swung in a vertical circle. At the bottom of the circle, the ball moves with a speed of 7.50 m/s.

m = 0.50 kgr = 1.20 mvbottom = 7.50 m/svtop = 4.00 m/sg = 9.8 m/s²
(a) Draw a free-body diagram of all forces on the ball at the bottom of the circle.

Sketch it on paper first, then compare.

Show the free-body diagram
(b) Calculate the centripetal force required to keep the ball moving in a circle.
N
Show solution

The required net inward force depends only on mass, speed and radius:

Fc = mv²/r = (0.50)(7.50)² / 1.20 = (0.50)(56.25) / 1.20 = 23.4 N

Direction: up, toward the center of the circle.

(c) Determine the tension in the string at the bottom of the circle.
N
Show solution

Take toward the center (up) as positive. Tension points toward the center (+). Weight points away from it (−).

T − mg = mv²/r T = mv²/r + mg = 23.4 + (0.50)(9.8) = 23.4 + 4.9 = 28.3 N

The string must pull harder than 23.4 N because it also has to overcome the ball's weight. That's why strings tend to break at the bottom.

(d) If the speed decreases to 4.00 m/s at the top of the circle, calculate the tension in the string at the top.
N
Show solution

At the top, the center is below the ball, so now both forces point toward the center:

T + mg = mv²/r mv²/r = (0.50)(4.00)² / 1.20 = 8.00 / 1.20 = 6.67 N T = 6.67 − 4.9 = 1.77 N

Gravity already supplies most of the 6.67 N needed, so the string only adds 1.77 N.

Going further: if the top speed dropped below √(gr) = √(9.8 × 1.20) ≈ 3.43 m/s, the tension would have to be negative, which is impossible. The string would go slack and the ball would leave the circle.

Summary Bottom: T − mg = mv²/r → T = 28.3 N (largest). Top: T + mg = mv²/r → T = 1.77 N (smallest). Same string, same ball, very different tensions, because gravity works against the string at the bottom and with it at the top.

Common mistakes

  • ✗ "Constant speed, so no acceleration."✓ The direction changes, so it accelerates.
  • ✗ Drawing acceleration outward or along v.✓ Acceleration always points to the center.
  • ✗ Adding an "Fc" arrow to the FBD.✓ Draw real forces only, then set their sum = mv²/r.
  • ✗ Forgetting to square v.✓ Double the speed means 4× the force.
  • ✗ Using the diameter for r.✓ r is center-to-object, half the diameter.
  • ✗ Plugging rpm straight in as f.✓ Divide rpm by 60 to get rev/s.
